The gap this closes

Sec 01.1 · Thesis

Routing engines are reactive by design. Crowd-sourced traffic needs enough probe vehicles to slow down, report in, and clear a confidence threshold before a link turns red on the map. On an arterial with thin probe coverage, that takes minutes. By the time the map knows, the vehicle is already committed to the turn pocket.

An autonomous vehicle already carries the sensors that would have told it earlier. The lidar returns and camera frames that keep the car in its lane also show the queue: how long it is, how fast it's growing, whether it's actually moving. That signal shows up locally, in seconds rather than minutes. It just isn't being used for routing yet.

Sixth Sense reads those same streams for operational intent instead of control intent, and turns them into three decisions: which link to take, where to wait, and when moving is worth the energy it costs.

One correction worth making up front

The original brief asked about conserving gas. Waymo's Austin fleet is the all-electric Jaguar I-PACE, and that changes more than the units. Regenerative braking recovers roughly two-thirds of the energy a stop would otherwise waste, so stop-and-go traffic is actually less punishing for this fleet than for a gas car. Aerodynamic drag scales with the square of speed, though, so freeway running is more punishing.

There's also a third cost a passenger car doesn't have. The sensor and compute stack draws roughly 1.2 kW whenever the vehicle is awake, plus 0.5 to 3.5 kW of climate load in an Austin summer. That draw gets billed per second, not per mile. A vehicle creeping along at 10 mph pays for it over six minutes to cover one mile; at 35 mph it pays for under two. So time in traffic is a direct energy cost even when the motor is barely working. That's why the optimum speed sits on a moving arterial, not on the freeway and not in the crawl.

Energy per mile vs. average speed: I-PACE with live AV stack Modeled

The curve makes the case on its own. Propulsion alone drops to a floor and then climbs with drag. Overhead, compute plus climate combined, behaves like a hyperbola: huge at low speed, almost nothing at freeway speed. Add them together and you get a shallow bowl with its floor around 30 to 35 mph.

In practice, a signal-coordinated Austin arterial held at 35 beats both the I-35 run at 65 and the downtown crawl at 12. The fleet's cheapest mile is the one that keeps moving at a moderate, predictable speed. That's exactly the mile the anticipation rules are trying to protect.

It also means idling to wait is never free. A vehicle staged on a curb with the stack awake burns about 0.02 kWh a minute and covers no distance at all. That number is the reason Rule C-01 exists.

What this is, and what it is not

Sec 01.3 · Boundaries

Advisory layer, explicitly outside the safety case

Sixth Sense biases route and dispatch cost. It does not command the vehicle. Every output is a weight handed to the planner, and the planner stays free to ignore it. No rule here can cause or prevent a maneuver. The safety-critical driving stack (perception, prediction, planning, the fallback behaviors) is untouched and stays in charge.

That line is drawn on purpose. A routing heuristic that can override a planner decision would inherit the planner's entire validation burden. Keeping this layer advisory means a bad threshold produces a slightly worse route, never an unsafe one, and lets the rules get tuned on real operational data at operational speed.

Where the numbers come from. Every figure on this page marked Modeled is generated from the assumptions stated in this document, not from Waymo, Uber, or City of Austin telemetry. None of that data is public at this level of detail. Street-level characteristics, service-area geography, and event constraints reflect real Austin conditions. The models are built so real data can replace the synthetic inputs without changing the underlying logic, and the Integration tab names the exact fields that would get swapped.

Austin has been working on this problem since 1985. Any model of where Waymos should go has to account for the system already on the street, the rail line already under construction, and a growing body of research on what actually happens when a private AV fleet moves into a transit city. Where our own model already agreed, and where it didn't

Sec 02.2 · Corridor overlay

We overlaid Route 801's actual path against the Corridors tab (§04), which was built independently and earlier for reasons that had nothing to do with transit policy: volatility, pedestrian conflict, curb access. Three of that route's segments were already scored AVOID: North Lamar, Guadalupe/The Drag, and South Congress. A model tuned to keep an AV off unpredictable, pedestrian-heavy streets landed, without being told to, on almost the exact spine Austin's busiest bus rapid transit line runs.

The overlay also turned up a real conflict, not just a coincidence. The Corridors tab's downtown default was the Lavaca/Guadalupe pair, the same streets that carry 801 and 803's dedicated transit-priority lanes through downtown. Recommending that pair as the default through-route put Sixth Sense's own logic in direct competition with the exact lanes the city built to keep buses out of car traffic.

Fixed as of this revision Applied. The downtown default now points to the Brazos/Colorado pair one block over, which the Corridors tab had already scored higher on curb availability for unrelated reasons. Lavaca/Guadalupe drops from an unconditional default to a conditional one: fine as through-traffic outside peak transit-priority hours, wrong as a place to stop and pick someone up.

What's coming: Project Connect

Sec 02.3 · 2029 horizon

Austin voters approved Project Connect in 2020. Phase 1 light rail (9.8 miles, 15 stops, running from 38th Street down through Downtown to East Riverside) is under construction now, alongside the future Orange Line (North Lamar to Stassney Lane) and Blue Line (Downtown to the airport). Both are targeting revenue service as early as 2029.

Two places this changes the model directly. East Riverside was already flagged in the Corridors tab as the highest re-survey priority in the set, because "light-rail construction is actively changing lane geometry." It turns out that note describes Phase 1's own alignment. It was written from street-level reasoning first, and this research pass later confirmed it names a real, dated project. ABIA Airport staging (SN-12) is currently justified by a long but predictable deadhead from the core, and it gets a real competitor once the Blue Line opens. A rider choosing between a $2.50 rail fare and an Uber-dispatched Waymo for a downtown-to-airport trip is making a genuinely different decision than the one the current staging model assumes.

Does ride-hail help or hurt transit? It depends.

Sec 02.4 · Substitution research

The research doesn't agree with itself, and that disagreement is actually the useful part. A University of Kentucky study found that ride-hail entry cuts rail ridership about 1.3% a year and bus ridership about 1.7%. UC Davis found that more than half of ride-hail trips replace a more sustainable mode, with transit the single most common one displaced. Other work finds the opposite: Uber as a net complement for the average transit agency, lifting ridership roughly 5% after two years, with bus benefiting more than rail.

Here's the pattern that reconciles both findings. Substitution shows up on trips that compete directly with a frequent line during its service hours. Complementarity shows up on trips transit doesn't cover well: after last call, before the first bus, or from a stop that's a 12-minute walk from anywhere. That's the split this project should actually run on, not a blanket stance in either direction.

A fifth rule family, added from this research

Families A through D predate this research pass. The substitution evidence above justified adding a new one, now live in the Rulebook (§05) as Family E · Transit awareness:

E-01 · TRANSIT-AWARE DISPATCH Added

"If a frequent bus or train covers this exact trip right now, I'm probably not the fastest or cheapest option, so let the app know."

Trigger: pickup and dropoff both within 400m of an 801/803/Rail stop pair, with a scheduled departure inside the rider's stated wait tolerance. Action: no cost penalty. This is a dispatch-time signal to the rider-facing app, not a routing constraint on the vehicle.

The empty-miles problem

Sec 02.5 · A real tension, not a solved one

This project's staging premise has a real cost attached to it

Independent analysis of California robotaxi filings puts Waymo's miles-traveled multiplier (total miles driven per passenger mile delivered) at 1.71, compared to 1.49 for New York's existing high-volume for-hire fleet. Roughly 44 to 45% of Waymo's California miles carry no passenger at all. NACTO has warned that, left unmanaged, this is exactly how AVs increase citywide VMT and undermine transit even while individual rides feel efficient to the person taking them.

Pre-positioning doesn't get a pass here. The Staging (§06) and Live Coverage (§07) tabs exist to cut rider wait time by moving idle vehicles toward anticipated demand before a trip is even requested, but every one of those repositioning miles is, structurally, the same empty mile this research is describing. Rule C-01 ("never circle, park stationary instead") and the Live Coverage anti-redundancy check ("don't send a second car to a zone that already has one committed") are real mitigations, but they don't make the tradeoff disappear. DEMAND_PER_VEHICLE is the actual dial here. Raise it and the fleet holds a thinner, more reactive posture: fewer deadhead miles, but longer rider waits. Lower it and coverage gets proactive, at a real cost in energy and congestion. A production deployment needs that dial set by a measured VMT budget, not a guess, and it needs to report its own multiplier the way Waymo's regulatory filings already do.

Who isn't being served today

Sec 02.6 · Equity

Northwest and Southwest Austin have bus stops more than a 10-minute walk from where people actually live and work. Lower-income households in the metro travel 10 to 15 miles farther per day than higher-income ones, and a transit trip commonly takes four to five times as long as the same trip by car. That's exactly the gap CapMetro's Equifare discount program and its Equitable Transit-Oriented Development policy exist to close.

Here's the complication, honestly: nothing about Sixth Sense as specified closes that gap on its own. Uber-dispatched Waymo pricing isn't need-based, and a rulebook optimized for operational efficiency and rider satisfaction has no built-in reason to prioritize a transit desert over a high-margin downtown corridor. Left alone, it will do the opposite, because that's where the demand and the fares are.

If closing the access gap is a real goal and not just a side effect, it has to be underwritten on purpose: a subsidized first/last-mile fare tied to Equifare eligibility, or a standing coverage floor for NW/SW Austin zones even where DEMAND_PER_VEHICLE alone wouldn't justify one. That's a pricing and policy decision, not something the routing math resolves by itself.

Demand index by zone and time block

Sec 02.2 · Generator model Modeled

How it's calculated. Each zone carries an intensity from 0 to 10 for seven generator classes: office, nightlife, residential, campus, retail, transport, event. Each class carries its own demand profile across the six time blocks. Zone demand is the dot product of the two, normalized so the busiest cell on the grid reads 100. Values are printed in every cell so the reading never depends on color alone.

Ask which streets a Waymo should take and the honest answer is that it depends on six things at once. The Segment Efficiency Index makes that dependency explicit rather than burying it in a routing cost function nobody can read.

Segment Efficiency Index: composition

SEI = 100 − 10 × Σ (wᵢ · cᵢ)

where each cᵢ is scored 0–10, worse = higher

  volatility      w = 0.22  unpredictable delay
  pedConflict     w = 0.16  crossing / jaywalk load
  signalDelay     w = 0.18  stops per mile × cycle
  curbDeficit     w = 0.15  legal pickup availability
  energyIndex     w = 0.15  kWh/mi vs. fleet baseline
  turnExposure    w = 0.14  unprotected left burden

verdict thresholds
  SEI ≥ 70   PREFER
  45 – 69    CONDITIONAL
  SEI < 45   AVOID

Volatility is weighted highest on purpose. A corridor that is reliably slow can be planned around; a corridor that is fine four days in five and catastrophic on the fifth destroys ETA accuracy, and ETA accuracy is what riders actually rate. Predictability beats raw speed.

Curb deficit gets its own term because a fast corridor you can't legally stop on isn't really a fast corridor. It's a fast corridor plus a lap around the block, which adds 4 to 7 minutes of rider-visible delay and roughly 0.4 kWh.

AVOID rarely means never. Most avoided segments here are high-demand destinations. The point is to avoid them as through-routes and approach them only on the final block, which is exactly what the parallel-pair recommendations in the notes column encode.

Every rule below starts as a sentence a driver would say out loud, and ends as a threshold a machine can evaluate. If a rule cannot survive that translation it does not belong in the book.

Rule A-01 in the field: the scenario it started from

This is the scenario the whole system was built around. The vehicle intends to turn right at the next intersection. Over two consecutive signal cycles, its cameras watch the queue on that receiving approach grow: six cars, then nine, then twelve, without the front of the queue discharging normally.

The map still shows the link green. No probe vehicle has driven it slowly enough to trigger a report, and none will for another two minutes.

A human driver keeps going straight and turns at the next signal instead, losing one block but saving four minutes. Sixth Sense raises the traversal cost of that link by 4.2x for 180 seconds and lets the planner reach the same conclusion on its own. It then logs the firing so the threshold can be scored against what actually happened.

These thresholds are starting points, not findings. Each one is set to a value defensible from traffic-engineering first principles (saturation headway, storage ratio, cycle length), and every one is expected to move once it's scored against real firings. The Integration tab defines the precision/recall loop that does that scoring. Modeled

An idle vehicle is a decision, not a pause. The Staging Position Score ranks every legal waiting spot in the service area against the demand that's about to show up, the cost of reaching it, and whether the curb will actually work when a rider walks up.

Staging Position Score

SPS = 45·Dₗ + 25·R − 20·H − 10·C + E

  Dₗ  local demand      zone demand at t, 0–1
  R  reachability     Σ demand(z)·e^(−t/8)
  H  deadhead cost    min to demand centroid
  C  curb penalty     grade A=0 … D=1
  E  event bonus      0–12, egress-aware

travel time between nodes
  t = dist · 0.047
      + 3.5 if crosses I-35
      + 2.5 if crosses the river

Reachability decays exponentially with an 8-minute half-life, which is roughly the point where a rider's patience and the dispatch system's willingness to reassign both start to bite.

What's actually running on this tab

The coordination algorithm is real and live. It re-runs in your browser roughly every 1.5 seconds, recomputing demand, checking which zones sit below target, matching the nearest free vehicle to the largest gap, and refusing to send a second car to a zone that already has one en route. That refusal is the entire point of fleet-to-fleet awareness, and you can watch it happen in the ticker on the right.

What isn't real: the 48 vehicles are a simulated fleet, not Waymo's actual cars. No outside party has access to that telemetry, and this project is an independent concept, not affiliated with or endorsed by Waymo. The metric that would kill this if ignored

Reroute churn. A layer that fires often enough to change the route every 30 seconds will improve every metric above while making the ride feel erratic and the ETA feel dishonest. Cap advisories at one accepted reroute per 4 minutes per vehicle, and track how often that cap gets hit. If it's hitting often, the thresholds are too loose no matter what precision says.

Field intel log

Sec 06.5 · Human loop

The rules encode what a driver knows. The log is how the fleet keeps learning things no sensor will report: that a specific curb is blocked every Tuesday morning, that a construction detour has moved, that a venue changed its egress route. Ops staff and field technicians enter observations against a corridor and a rule, and those entries feed threshold tuning. Where a pattern holds steady, it becomes a scheduled constraint.

This demo stores entries in your browser only, so the page stays publicly shareable. In deployment the same form POSTs to /v1/field-intel with the corridor, window, rule id, observer id and free text, and entries become visible fleet-wide. The export button emits exactly that payload shape.
